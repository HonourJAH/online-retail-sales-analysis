# Findings log: Online Retail II

Write-ups for each business question. All figures are net of returns unless stated, and every query result
was reconciled to a trusted total. The queries are in `sql/analysis/`.

**Q1: Gross sales, returns and net sales.** Over Dec 2010 to Dec 2011, the business recorded gross sales of 10,001,566.12, returns of 230,247.96 and net sales of 9,771,318.16. Returns equal 2.30% of gross sales, so the large majority of revenue was retained.

**Q2: Monthly net sales trend.** Net sales moved between roughly 480K and 760K a month from December 2010 to August 2011, with the low points in February (499,464.35) and April (482,061.93). Sales then climbed from September, reaching 1.01M in September, 1.06M in October and a peak of 1.43M in November, a 34.42% rise on October. Those three months together account for about 35.8% of all net sales over the 13-month period. The pattern is consistent with seasonal demand ahead of Christmas, but the data doesn't state a cause. December 2011 contains only nine days of data (the file ends on 2011-12-09), so its -69.14% change is not a real decline. Returns are counted in the month the cancellation was recorded, not the month of the original sale.

**Q3: Revenue by country.** The United Kingdom generates 8,281,390.36 of net sales, 84.75% of the total, so the business is overwhelmingly domestic. The next five countries are the Netherlands (283,479.54, 2.90%), Ireland (259,380.02, 2.65%), Germany (200,619.66, 2.05%), France (182,076.60, 1.86%) and Australia (136,922.50, 1.40%), which together contribute 10.86%. Every other country is under 1% of net sales. The ranking includes three labels that are not single countries (Unspecified, European Community and Channel Islands), which together account for about 0.26%.

**Q4: Top products.** The Regency cakestand 3 tier is the top product by net revenue at 164,459.49 (1.68% of net sales), followed by the white hanging heart T-light holder (99,790.93) and party bunting (98,243.88). The top 10 products by revenue total 799,577.48, only 8.18% of net sales across 3,811 products, so revenue is spread widely and not concentrated in a few items. By units, the popcorn holder leads with 56,427, followed by the World War 2 gliders (53,751) and the jumbo bag red retrospot (47,256). Five products appear in both top-10 lists. The cakestand and picnic basket rank high on revenue because of their price (about 12.65 and 27.40 per unit) despite modest volume, while five of the top-10 units leaders sell for between 0.25 and 0.64 per unit and the popcorn holder, first on units, sells at about 0.90. Figures are net of returns.

**Q5: Return rates by product.** Across the dataset, 8,666 return lines against 522,535 sale lines is an overall line return rate of about 1.66%. Among the 1,550 products with at least 100 sale lines, the highest rates run from 10.71% to 16.23%, roughly 6 to 10 times the overall rate. The ceramic cake stand with hanging cakes is highest at 16.23% (25 return lines against 154 sale lines), followed by the red retrospot traditional teapot (14.48%) and the small glass cloche (12.16%). Each of the top 10 has between 16 and 35 return lines, with no single return larger than 36 units, so these are repeated returns and not one large order. Four of the ten are glass or ceramic items by name, but the data doesn't give return reasons, so no cause is claimed. Measuring returns by units instead would have ranked the rotating silver angels T-light holder first at 99.10%, but that comes from one 9,360-unit cancellation (invoice C536757) with no matching sale of that size in the dataset, so the line-based rate is the more reliable measure.

**Q6: Customers and order value.** 4,333 identified customers placed at least one sale, across 19,771 sale orders, about 4.2 orders per identified customer. Another 1,371 orders (6.9% of orders) carry no customer ID, so they count as sales but cannot be attributed to a customer. The average gross order value is 505.87, but the median is 302.20, so a typical order is considerably smaller than the average suggests, and the average is pulled up by a smaller number of very large orders, the biggest being 52,940.94. Values are gross of returns, since cancellations are not orders.

**Q7: Revenue concentration.** Customer-level analysis covers identified customers only: 15.41% of net revenue has no customer ID and can't be attributed, which leaves 8,265,476.93 across 4,361 customers (4,333 with a sale, plus 28 who appear only through cancellations). The top customer, 14646, contributes 278,778.02 (3.37%), followed by 18102 at 259,657.30 (3.14%), and the top 10 customers together account for 16.59% of identified revenue, about 14.0% of all net sales. Revenue is moderately concentrated: 234 customers, 5.37% of the base, generate half of identified revenue, and the top 10% of customers (437) generate 60.36%. No single customer exceeds 3.4%, so the risk lies in a core group, not one account.

**Q8: Busiest days and hours.** Thursday is the busiest day, with 4,209 orders and 2,131,596.38 in gross sales, followed by Wednesday and Tuesday by orders. Sunday is the quietest, with 2,203 orders and 798,311.50. The data has no Saturday orders at all, so trading ran Sunday to Friday, and the data doesn't say why Saturday is missing. By hour, activity peaks at noon (3,205 orders, 1,412,921.32), and the hours from 10:00 to 15:59 account for about 78% of orders and 75% of gross sales. Orders ramp up from 8:00, fall away sharply after 16:00, and the last orders are at 20:00. Times are as recorded in the data, and the time zone isn't stated. Hourly order counts add to one more than the daily total because invoice 549245 has lines in two different hours and is counted in both. Gross sales exclude cancellations.

**Q9: RFM segmentation.** 4,333 identified customers were scored on recency (days since last purchase, measured to 2011-12-10), frequency (distinct sale orders) and monetary value (gross spend). Recency was scored in quintiles. Frequency used fixed bands (1, 2, 3 to 4, 5 to 10, and 11 or more orders) because 34.7% of customers placed exactly one order, and quintiles would have split identical customers into different scores. Champions, 816 customers (18.8%), generate 63.2% of identified gross revenue (5,366,220.79), about 6,576 per customer, and averaged 12.4 orders with a last purchase 13 days before the end of the data. Champions and Loyal customers together are 24.9% of customers and 72.5% of revenue. At the other end, Lost customers (847, 19.5%) average 270 days since their last purchase and produce 5.2% of revenue, about 521 per customer. At Risk is small (20 customers, 36,911.22 in revenue) but consists of frequent buyers (7.6 orders on average) who have gone quiet, which makes them the clearest win-back candidates. The segment rules are an analyst's judgement, and because the data covers only 13 months, customers who buy once a year may appear as Lost or About to Sleep.

**Q10: Cohort retention.** Identified customers were grouped by the month of their first purchase in the data, and retention was measured as the share of each cohort that placed at least one sale order in a later calendar month. Among the January to October 2011 cohorts, 14.8% to 23.5% of new customers bought again the following month, and the share active six months after their first purchase was between 21.7% and 26.8% for the cohorts old enough to measure. Retention doesn't decay steadily after the first month: it stays in a band of roughly 17% to 30%, so customers who return tend to remain active. The December 2010 cohort (884 customers, 36.5% in month 1) is not comparable, because the data starts on 2010-12-01 and many of those customers were probably established before then. The September and October cohorts returned slightly more in the first months (23.4% and 23.5% in month 1), which coincides with the autumn sales peak, but the data does not show why. December 2011 has only nine days of data, so retention is reported only up to November 2011. Retention here means being active in a given calendar month, so a customer who skips a month and returns later is counted only in the months they bought.
